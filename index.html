Competitor Pricing (optional)

If your competitors charge a different price, your customers may notice — and that affects how many choose you. This section adjusts your starting demand estimate based on your price relative to the market.

Why this is separate from elasticity: Elasticity tells you what happens when you change price. This tells you what happens when your price is different from the market. If everyone raises prices together, only elasticity applies. If you raise alone, both apply.

Leave blank to skip this adjustment entirely.
0.5 = customers are somewhat sensitive to the gap.
1.0 = very sensitive. 0.2 = mostly loyal to you.

Worked example:
You charge $55. Competitors average $50. Gap = +10% above market.
Sensitivity = 0.5 → demand reduced by 0.5 × 10% = −5%.

Now suppose you lower to $45 (−10% below market): demand boosted by +5% → starts from 105.
This adjustment is applied to your base quantity before anything else runs.
Kinked Demand — What it is & how to use it

The core idea: In many markets, raising your price loses customers faster than lowering your price gains them. Competitors will happily undercut you if you lower, but won't raise with you — so the demand curve has a "kink" at your current price.

Real example: A personal trainer charges $60/session. When they raised to $70, they lost 3 of 20 clients (−15% demand, elasticity ≈ −0.8). When they'd previously dropped to $50, they gained only 2 clients (+10% demand, elasticity ≈ −1.6). The market punished cutting far less than it rewarded raising — a classic kink.

When to use kinked demand: You're in a competitive local market (gym, cleaner, tutor, trades). Competitors match your cuts but not your raises. Your customers are loyal but price-aware.
When not to use it: You're the only provider, or your customers are not price-sensitive at all — use power-law instead.

Arc Elasticity Calculator

Uses the arc (midpoint) formula. For price changes ≤20%, error vs. the power-law model is <1% — safe to use directly. For larger changes (>25%), the implied power-law curve may not perfectly reproduce your historical data point; treat the result as approximate.

Model Notes

Power-law demand: Q = A · Pe — constant elasticity across all prices. Default. Works well for most service businesses.
Linear demand: Q = A + b·P — derived from elasticity at base point. Demand hits zero at a finite price. Use when you believe response is roughly proportional.
Important: For inelastic demand (|e| < 1), power-law suggests very high optimal prices with no upper bound. This is mathematically correct but ignores brand limits, competitor ceilings, and psychological thresholds. Use the floor price and your judgment.
Kinked demand: Two power-law arms anchored at your current price. Use a less-negative elasticity for the raising-price arm and a more-negative one for the lowering-price arm. Models markets where competitors don't follow price cuts. Update base price after each real-world test.
All outputs are scenarios, not predictions. ↓/↑ demand bands = 5th/95th percentile from 1,000 Monte Carlo trials at a fixed price (if you don't reprice).

How the Simulator Works

Simple Mode vs Advanced Mode

Simple Mode is for business owners who want a quick answer. It uses your price, customer count, and business type to estimate a suggested price range in plain English. The Step 2 wizard lets you calibrate how sensitive your customers are to price from a past price change — highly recommended. Advanced Mode exposes all parameters for detailed analysis.

What "Scenario" Means

Every number this tool produces is a modelled scenario, not a prediction. The results depend on your estimate of how sensitive your customers are to price — which is itself uncertain. The price range is the direction to explore; the single "optimal price" is a starting point for a real-world test, not a guaranteed outcome.

Calibration — Why 3+ Points?

With exactly 2 data points, the regression always returns R²=1.0 — a perfect fit by definition. This is a mathematical artifact, not evidence of reliability. Three or more points give a genuine goodness-of-fit measure.

Real Data Sanity Check

Enter a past price change and what happened to your customers. The tool calculates the implied sensitivity from your real experience and warns you if it diverges significantly from what you've entered. One data point can be affected by seasonality or other factors — treat it as a sanity check, not a definitive calibration.

Two Uncertainty Bands (Advanced)

These two bands answer different real-world questions about risk:

Held-price band (↓/↑ demand): "What's my profit range if I set this price and then do nothing?" Demand is uncertain — this band shows the pessimistic (5th percentile) to optimistic (95th percentile) outcomes if you hold your price fixed no matter what demand actually comes in.

Re-optimised band: "What if I could adjust my price after seeing actual demand?" This is the best profit you could achieve by repricing to match what customers actually want. It's always at least as good as the held-price band. The gap between the two is the value of staying flexible and adjusting.

Both bands are 5th/95th percentile outcomes from 1,000 Monte Carlo trials. The Qty Uncertainty % input controls how wide the demand swings are.

Demand Models

Power-law (default): constant elasticity across all price levels. Linear: demand response scales proportionally from your base point. Kinked demand: two separate elasticities — one for price increases, one for decreases. Models the real-world observation that raising your price loses more customers than lowering it gains (or vice versa). Both arms are anchored to your current price/quantity so the demand curve is continuous at the kink point.
